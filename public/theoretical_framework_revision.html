Part II — Revised Theoretical Framework

(The following section represents the revised, academic draft for Section 1.2 "Theoretical Framework" in Chapter 1 of the thesis manuscript.)

This study is grounded in a thematic theoretical framework composed of five core pillars: Digital Signal Processing and Optical Sensing Theory, Computer Vision and Directional Ingress/Egress Counting Theory, Supervised Machine Learning Pipeline Theory, Spatial Trilateration and Constrained Network Routing Theory, and Socio-Technical Quality and Acceptance Theory. Rather than presenting abstract mathematical formulations of third-party libraries, this framework focuses on the scientific principles and practical engineering equations that directly govern how raw environmental signals are captured by the FlowJeep hardware, processed by our algorithms, mapped onto transit networks, and evaluated for public utility vehicle deployment.

1. Digital Signal Processing and Optical Sensing Theory

The physical data acquisition layer of the FlowJeep vehicle unit operates on the principles of optoelectronic sensing, finite-state directional decoding, and event-driven sensor fusion. Detecting passenger boarding and alighting within the confined rear doorway of a traditional or modern jeepney requires transducing physical human motion into discrete digital signals under strict electrical power, memory, and bandwidth constraints.

Under photoelectric beam-interruption theory, an active infrared sensor (E18-D80NK) continuously projects modulated optical radiation in the near-infrared band (880–950 nm) and monitors the reflected return using an internal photodiode paired with a synchronous demodulator (Radovan et al., 2024). When an entering or exiting passenger breaks the optical threshold, radiant flux reflection triggers an internal open-collector NPN switching transistor from cutoff into saturation. Tied to a 3.3 V pull-up rail, this transition generates a binary logic pulse, x[n] ∈ {0, 1}, captured as an asynchronous hardware interrupt by the ESP32-CAM microcontroller.

To resolve passenger movement direction while providing spatial count verification, the physical sensor assembly is mounted in an inline sequence at the jeepney doorway threshold:

Physical Doorway Layout:    [ IR Sensor 1 (IR1) ]  ——  [ OV3660 Camera (CAM) ]  ——  [ IR Sensor 2 (IR2) ]

Under this physical geometry, the two active infrared sensors, IR1 and IR2, are separated by a spatial baseline distance Δd, with the OV3660 camera module centered between them. Under single-passenger transit at doorway walking velocity vp, a passenger trips the sensors sequentially, generating two falling-edge hardware interrupts separated by transit duration Δt ≈ Δd / vp (Jurak et al., 2023). A Finite State Machine (FSM) implemented in firmware decodes directionality: an interrupt sequence of IR1 → IR2 indicates passenger boarding (inflow), whereas IR2 → IR1 indicates passenger alighting (outflow).

However, optical beam-break sensing suffers from a well-documented failure mode in high-density paratransit: simultaneous parallel crossings (Radovan et al., 2024). When two commuters board side-by-side or closely tail one another through the doorway, both individuals obstruct the beam concurrently. Because photoelectric sensors output a one-dimensional binary state, this simultaneous crossing collapses into a single continuous pulse, causing infrared-only systems to systematically undercount passenger volume during rush hours.

To resolve this limitation without the prohibitive power and data costs of continuous cellular video streaming, FlowJeep implements an event-triggered multi-modal sensor fusion architecture (Radovan et al., 2024). The inline layout IR1 — CAM — IR2 functions as an asynchronous hardware trigger gate. The OV3660 camera remains in low-power standby until an optical beam is broken. Tripping either IR1 or IR2 immediately triggers an edge interrupt that commands the ESP32-CAM to capture a single JPEG doorway snapshot. This image is transmitted over the Luat Air780E LTE Cat-1 cellular module to the Flask backend for computer vision verification. The low-latency optical signal provides millisecond-level event detection, while the spatial snapshot provides multi-person separation. If visual analysis detects multiple passenger bounding boxes crossing the doorway during the event window, the vision count overrides the single optical tally, directly correcting the parallel-passenger failure mode.

2. Computer Vision and Directional Ingress/Egress Counting Theory

When a snapshot is received by the backend server, the image is represented as a spatial color tensor I ∈ ℜH × W × C. Extracting an accurate passenger count from this frame requires two stages: deep convolutional person detection and virtual line-crossing state tracking.

For object detection, the system applies the established Single-Stage Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) paradigm, utilizing the off-the-shelf YOLOv8n (Nano) model developed by Ultralytics (Jocher et al., 2023). Unlike two-stage detectors that generate region proposals before classification, YOLO formulates detection as a single-pass spatial regression problem, predicting bounding box coordinates b = [xc, yc, w, h]T and class confidence scores in one forward inference pass. FlowJeep leverages this pre-trained neural network off-the-shelf, filtering detections strictly to COCO Class 0 (person) to isolate human silhouettes.

To reject non-passenger false positives (such as cargo, bags, or roadside clutter visible through an open door), the codebase implements geometric plausibility filtering in tracker.py based on human body aspect ratios:

\(\text{Aspect Ratio } AR = \frac{h}{w}, \quad \text{Condition: } AR_{\text{min}} \le AR \le AR_{\text{max}} \quad \text{and} \quad w \ge w_{\text{min}}, \ h \ge h_{\text{min}}\)

Candidates failing these dimensional thresholds or falling below the detection confidence threshold (conf < 0.45) are discarded before tracking association.

For maintaining target identities across sequential frames, the backend incorporates the off-the-shelf ByteTrack algorithm (Zhang et al., 2022) via the Ultralytics tracking engine (bytetrack.yaml). ByteTrack associates incoming bounding boxes with existing trajectories using an internal Kalman filter and Hungarian bipartite matching, preserving track continuity even during temporary visual occlusions without requiring custom tracking classes in the application code.

To translate tracked passenger trajectories into discrete ingress and egress counts, the system implements Virtual Line Crossing and Hysteresis Band Theory in counter.py. A horizontal virtual boundary line Ly is established across the doorway frame. To eliminate false count triggers caused by vehicle vibration, passenger hesitation, or foot shuffling, a deadband hysteresis zone [Ly − δ, Ly + δ] is enforced around the threshold. The centroid y-coordinate of each tracked person, cy = (y1 + y2) / 2, is mapped to a discrete spatial state:

\(\text{State}(c_y) = \begin{cases} \text{ABOVE}, & \text{if } c_y < L_y - \delta \\ \text{BELOW}, & \text{if } c_y > L_y + \delta \\ \text{DEADZONE}, & \text{if } L_y - \delta \le c_y \le L_y + \delta \end{cases}\)

A crossing event is logged only when a track exhibits a monotonic transition between spatial states across the deadband. In the FlowJeep doorway orientation (BELOW_TO_ABOVE), state transitions generate discrete event tallies:

\(\text{Transition} = \begin{cases} \text{ENTRY (Inflow)}, & \text{if } \text{State}_{\text{prev}} = \text{BELOW} \ \land \ \text{State}_{\text{curr}} = \text{ABOVE} \\ \text{EXIT (Outflow)}, & \text{if } \text{State}_{\text{prev}} = \text{ABOVE} \ \land \ \text{State}_{\text{curr}} = \text{BELOW} \end{cases}\)

The instantaneous onboard passenger load Pcurr is computed by the running balance equation:

\(P_{\text{curr}} = \max\left(0, P_{\text{initial}} + \sum \text{ENTRY} - \sum \text{EXIT}\right)\)

This running occupancy is mapped into the three-tier passenger load status indicator (Available, Near-Full, Full) displayed to commuters on the mobile interface.

3. Supervised Machine Learning Pipeline Theory

The predictive component of the FlowJeep system addresses the stochastic, non-linear variation of paratransit passenger demand using Supervised Machine Learning Pipeline Theory. Commuter demand along urban jeepney corridors is shaped by complex interactions between operational schedules, temporal peak periods, and adverse meteorological conditions that traditional parametric models fail to capture (Rashvand & Gholami, 2025).

The prediction workflow follows a multi-stage machine learning pipeline consisting of feature ingestion, tabular encoding, hypothesis training, and generalization evaluation. The prediction target Y ∈ [0, Cmax] is a continuous variable representing the expected passenger load along a specific route segment. In accordance with the feature architecture defined in forecastService.ts and backend schemas, the input feature vector x combines cyclical temporal parameters with live atmospheric observations retrieved programmatically via weather APIs:

\(\mathbf{x} = \big[ x_{\text{tod}}, \ x_{\text{dow}}, \ x_{\text{hist}}, \ x_{\text{temp}}, \ x_{\text{hum}}, \ x_{\text{cond}} \big]^T\)

where xtod represents the time-of-day dispatch window (spanning six standardized operational periods: Early Worker Dispatch, Morning Peak Rush, Midday Off-Peak, Afternoon School Surge, Evening Homeward Rush, and Night Sweep), xdow represents day-of-week classification, xhist is the historical moving average ridership for that segment, and xtemp, xhum, and xcond represent ambient temperature, relative humidity, and categorical weather condition codes (Rashvand & Gholami, 2025).

The predictive engine employs the Random Forest Regression algorithm (Breiman, 2001) implemented via the scikit-learn Python package. Random Forest constructs an ensemble of B independent regression trees trained on bootstrap samples of the field dataset, with random feature subspace splitting at each node. By aggregating predictions across multiple uncorrelated trees, the ensemble averages out individual tree variance, producing robust passenger load predictions even when early operational training datasets are small. Model accuracy is evaluated against field-observer ground truth during system trials using the standard regression metrics:

\(\text{MAE} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N |Y_i - \hat{Y}_i|, \quad \text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N (Y_i - \hat{Y}_i)^2}, \quad R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum (Y_i - \hat{Y}_i)^2}{\sum (Y_i - \bar{Y})^2}\)

with an operational acceptance threshold established at R2 ≥ 0.50 for prototype validation.

4. Spatial Trilateration, Map Matching, and Geometric Network Routing Theory

Real-time vehicle tracking, route path rendering, and dynamic Estimated Time of Arrival (ETA) computations are grounded in GNSS Trilateration, Spatial Map-Matching Theory, and Network Routing Principles.

Geospatial positioning is acquired by the on-board u-blox NEO-6M GNSS receiver, which computes geodetic latitude φ, longitude λ, speed vg, and heading angle θ through satellite pseudorange trilateration. The module streams NMEA 0183 sentences (`$GPRMC`) over serial UART to the ESP32-CAM, which parses and relays coordinates to Firebase Realtime Database over the Air780E cellular link (Costa et al., 2023). In high-density urban corridors, multipath reflection from commercial buildings and elevated infrastructure degrades satellite fix quality, introducing horizontal positional errors εh ~ CEP of 7 to 13 meters for consumer-grade GNSS modules (Tomaštík et al., 2022).

The positional discrepancy between the system-reported vehicle coordinates and field-verified ground-truth locations is quantified during hardware testing using the spherical Haversine distance formula:

\(d_{\text{Haversine}} = 2R \arcsin \left( \sqrt{\sin^2\left(\frac{\Delta \phi}{2}\right) + \cos(\phi_1)\cos(\phi_2)\sin^2\left(\frac{\Delta \lambda}{2}\right)} \right)\)

where R ≈ 6,371,000 meters, Δφ = φ2 − φ1, and Δλ = λ2 − λ1.

Because public utility vehicles travel strictly along physical roadway centerlines rather than unconstrained Euclidean space, raw commuter destination pins and GPS coordinates must be snapped to valid corridor paths. The system applies Map-Matching and Route-Constrained Geospatial Snapping Theory. In src/utils/jeepneyCorridors.ts, the transit route is represented as a sequence of road segments Sj = [a, b] defined by OpenStreetMap vector coordinates.

When a commuter selects a destination point p = [lat, lng], the point is projected onto each candidate segment using the orthogonal point-to-segment projection implemented in getClosestPointOnSegment():

\(t = \text{clamp}\left( \frac{(x - x_1)\Delta x + (y - y_1)\Delta y}{\Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2}, \ 0, \ 1 \right), \quad \text{where } \Delta x = x_2 - x_1, \ \Delta y = y_2 - y_1\)
\(\text{Closest Point } p^* = \big[ x_1 + t \cdot \Delta x, \ y_1 + t \cdot \Delta y \big]\)

The physical offset distance from the commuter's selected pin to the snapped roadside stopping point is computed using local planar equirectangular projection:

\(d_{\text{offset}} = \sqrt{\big[(p[0] - p^*[0]) \cdot K_{\text{lat}}\big]^2 + \big[(p[1] - p^*[1]) \cdot K_{\text{lng}}\big]^2}\)

where Klat = 111,320 meters/degree and Klng = 111,320 · cos(lat · π / 180). To ensure rapid execution across hundreds of corridor segments, segment bounding boxes are indexed client-side using an R-Tree spatial index (RBush), optimizing candidate query performance to O(log N).

Dynamic ETA to the snapped stopping point p* is calculated by integrating the vehicle’s instantaneous GPS velocity with historical segment travel profiles:

\(\text{ETA}(p^*) = \sum_{k \in \text{path}} \left( \alpha \frac{d_k}{v_{\text{live}}} + (1 - \alpha) \bar{\tau}_k \right) + \sum_{s \in \text{stops}} \tau_{\text{dwell}}(s)\)

where dk is segment distance, vlive is current GPS speed, τ̄k is historical segment duration, α is a speed-smoothing weighting factor, and τdwell is stop boarding delay (Jeong et al., 2024). This prevents misleading ETA spikes during brief stops at intersections.

5. Socio-Technical Quality and Acceptance Theory

The final theoretical pillar governs the evaluation of FlowJeep as an integrated engineering prototype deployed within a human commuter ecosystem. Evaluating paratransit technology requires two distinct measurement paradigms: objective software engineering product quality and commuter behavioral adoption.

Technical system validation is grounded in the ISO/IEC 25010:2023 Software Product Quality Model (SQuaRE) (International Organization for Standardization, 2023). Five standardized quality characteristics are operationalized into concrete, testable engineering benchmarks:

  • Functional Suitability: Evaluates whether system outputs correctly match physical realities, evaluated through GPS Haversine error (≤ 13 m ceiling), passenger counting accuracy against manual observer ground truth, and Random Forest load prediction goodness-of-fit (R2 ≥ 0.50).
  • Performance Efficiency: Measures latency and throughput, benchmarked by cellular snapshot upload latency (≤ 2 s ceiling) over the Luat Air780E module, YOLOv8n inference frame processing time, and Firebase-to-mobile display synchronization latency.
  • Reliability: Evaluates operational availability, measured via automatic cellular reconnection recovery under spotty network corridors and MQTT packet delivery success rates.
  • Security: Evaluates data protection and compliance with Republic Act No. 10173 (Philippine Data Privacy Act of 2012) via authenticated Firebase token exchanges and the strict non-storage of commuter facial biometric identifiers.
  • Usability: Evaluates commuter interface comprehensibility, operability, and user satisfaction across the mobile application surfaces.

Commuter adoption dynamics are theoretically framed by the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), formulated by Davis (1989). TAM posits that the behavioral intention (BI) of users to adopt a new technology is fundamentally governed by two core cognitive constructs: Perceived Usefulness (PU) and Perceived Ease of Use (PEOU):

\(PEOU \rightarrow PU \quad \text{and} \quad \{PU, PEOU\} \rightarrow BI \rightarrow \text{Actual System Adoption} \quad \text{\textit{(Davis, 1989)}}\)

In FlowJeep's evaluation, PU measures whether commuters perceive real-time tracking, occupancy alerts, and demand signaling as genuinely beneficial in reducing transit wait uncertainty. PEOU measures whether the mobile interface is operable without prior instruction. Evaluated through six-item Likert subscales administered during field trials, commuter ratings are tested against an inferential acceptance ceiling (μ0 ≥ 3.40, "Good" threshold), providing empirical validation of paratransit system adoption.
